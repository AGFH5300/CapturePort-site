How to Write a Site Inspection Report

For construction inspectors, site engineers, facilities managers, and project supervisors  ·  Updated May 2026

A site inspection report documents the condition, progress, compliance, or safety status of a construction site, building, or infrastructure project at a specific point in time. It is a professional record that protects all parties, drives accountability, and provides the evidence base for decisions, disputes, and handovers.

This guide explains exactly what a site inspection report should contain, how to structure it, and how to capture evidence efficiently in the field — including in environments where connectivity is unreliable.

What is a site inspection report?

A site inspection report is a formal document produced by an engineer, project manager, inspector, or facilities professional after physically attending a site to assess conditions, progress, compliance, or defects. It serves as the official record of that visit.

Common types include:

  • Construction progress inspection report
  • Pre-handover or snagging inspection report
  • Safety inspection report
  • Structural or civil inspection report
  • Defect liability inspection report
  • Facilities maintenance inspection report
  • Insurance or loss adjustment site inspection report

Section by section: what to write

Project and site details

Record the project name, full site address, client name, main contractor, and any relevant subcontractors. Include the contract reference or project number. Note who was present during the inspection — the site manager, contractor representative, or client representative. This information is critical if the report is ever referenced in a dispute.

Weather and site conditions

Record the weather at the time of inspection. This is more important than it appears. Concrete pours, external rendering, waterproofing applications, and structural assessments can all be affected by temperature and humidity. If conditions limited the scope of your inspection, say so.

Scope of inspection

State clearly which areas, floors, trades, or systems you inspected, and which you did not. If access was restricted (locked areas, safety exclusion zones, works in progress), record this. Never imply broader coverage than you actually conducted.

Protecting yourself: A scope limitation is not a weakness in your report — it is a professional statement. "The roof area was not inspected due to the absence of safe access equipment at the time of attendance" is far better than a report that omits the roof without explanation.

Findings by area

Organise findings geographically or by trade — whichever is clearer for the specific project. For each area:

  • State what you observed (condition, progress status, non-conformance)
  • Reference the relevant specification, drawing number, or standard where applicable
  • Note the photo reference numbers that support the finding
  • Distinguish between defects that require immediate action and observations for monitoring

Photo appendix

Never submit a site inspection report without photographic evidence. Findings without photos are easy to dispute. Best practices:

  • Take an orientation shot of each area before close-up detail shots
  • Caption every photo at the time of capture with location and what is being shown
  • Number photos sequentially and reference them in the findings section by number
  • Ensure photos are in focus and well-lit — use a torch in confined spaces if necessary

Action items

List each required action clearly. State who is responsible (contractor, subcontractor, client, inspector), and give a target completion date. Vague actions ("defects to be addressed") are unenforceable. Specific actions ("Main contractor to rectify damp penetration at Grid B/3 ground floor and notify inspector for re-inspection before closing up — target: 15 June") are actionable.

The field capture problem

Site inspections are physical, fast-moving, and often chaotic. The common failure is not in the report structure — it is in evidence capture. Inspectors leave site with:

  • Dozens of uncaptioned photos that make no sense three hours later
  • Handwritten notes that are illegible or incomplete
  • Voice memos that are never transcribed

The report then gets written from incomplete memory, often late at night after a long site day. The result is a weaker, less accurate document than the inspection itself deserves.

The solution: Caption photos and record observations immediately at each location, before moving to the next area. The thirty seconds spent captioning a photo on site saves thirty minutes of reconstruction at the office — and produces a more accurate, defensible record.

Common mistakes in site inspection reports

  • No scope statement — readers assume you inspected everything if you do not state what you inspected and excluded
  • Uncaptioned photos — 40 photos with no captions or references are not evidence, they are a gallery
  • Vague action items — action items without a responsible party and a date are rarely completed
  • Missing weather/conditions data — critical for construction-related reports where environmental factors affect findings
  • Late delivery — a site inspection report delivered a week after the visit loses credibility and may miss time-sensitive issues
  • Speculative causation — report what you observed. Avoid speculating on cause unless you are qualified to do so and have conducted the appropriate investigation

Site inspection report template (section headings)

  1. Report reference and inspection date
  2. Prepared by (name, qualifications, company)
  3. Project details (name, address, client, contractor)
  4. Personnel present
  5. Weather and site conditions
  6. Scope of inspection
  7. Findings — by area or trade
  8. Photo appendix
  9. Action items (action, responsible party, target date)
  10. Remarks and limitations
  11. Signature and date

Frequently asked questions

Who can write a site inspection report?

Any qualified professional who has attended the site — engineer, project manager, surveyor, facilities manager, or inspector. The level of qualification required depends on the purpose. Statutory inspections (structural, fire safety) require appropriate professional accreditation. Routine progress and snagging reports do not.

How often should site inspections be conducted?

This depends on the project stage, contract requirements, and risk level. Active construction sites are typically inspected weekly at minimum. Critical stages (foundations, structure, waterproofing, fit-out handover) require inspection at each key milestone, regardless of frequency schedule.

Should a site inspection report include a checklist?

Checklists are useful for routine maintenance inspections where the same items are assessed each visit. For construction and defect inspections, a structured narrative with photographic evidence is more appropriate and more defensible. Use both where the contract or client requires it.
