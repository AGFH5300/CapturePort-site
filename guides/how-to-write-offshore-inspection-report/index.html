How to Write an Offshore Inspection Report

For offshore inspectors, service engineers, and equipment surveyors  ·  Updated May 2026

An offshore inspection report documents the condition, performance, or compliance status of equipment, systems, or structures on an offshore platform, floating production unit, or offshore support vessel. The offshore environment — restricted connectivity, strict safety protocols, limited turnaround time, and high-value assets — demands reporting that is accurate, complete, and delivered promptly.

Characteristics that make offshore reports different

Offshore inspection reports share the structure of onshore inspection reports but have several important differences:

  • No internet on location — most offshore platforms have restricted or expensive satellite data. Your reporting tool must work fully offline.
  • Safety-critical context — findings on an offshore platform can have immediate safety implications. Severity classifications must be used consistently and conservatively.
  • Tight turnaround — operators need reports quickly to make operational decisions. A report delivered 48 hours after leaving the platform has diminished value.
  • High stakes evidence — offshore inspection reports may be required by regulatory bodies (NOPSEMA, HSE, BSEE), operators, insurers, and class societies. The evidence standard must withstand scrutiny.
  • Permit to Work references — in many cases, inspections are conducted under a Permit to Work (PTW) system. Reference the relevant PTW number in your report.

Standard report structure

  • Report reference, date, and prepared by
  • Facility/vessel name, location, operator, and installation type
  • Inspection scope and basis (standards, codes, client specification)
  • Personnel attended and qualifications
  • Permit to Work reference(s)
  • Equipment or system inspected — tag numbers, asset references
  • Findings — by component or system, with condition rating
  • Defect list — numbered, with severity and recommended action
  • Measurements and test results
  • Photo appendix
  • Recommendations — prioritised by safety risk
  • Signature and qualifications

Tag numbers and asset references

Offshore facilities operate rigorous asset management systems. Every piece of inspectable equipment has a tag number in the operator's computerised maintenance management system (CMMS). Your report must reference these tag numbers — not just descriptions. "HP separator V-301" is clear; "HP separator, west side of process deck" is not sufficient in an offshore context.

Severity classification for offshore defects

Use a severity system that reflects the offshore safety context. A common approach:

  • Safety Critical — defect presents immediate risk to personnel, environment, or asset integrity. Requires immediate stop-work or shutdown decision. Must be verbally notified to the installation manager before leaving the area.
  • Urgent — significant risk. Action required before next planned inspection or within a defined short timeframe (e.g. 7–14 days). Written notification to client within 24 hours.
  • Required — defect to be corrected at next planned maintenance opportunity. Include in the CMMS as a corrective work order.
  • Recommended — good practice action, not immediately necessary. Include as a recommendation in the report.

Verbal notification for safety-critical findings: Never leave a safety-critical finding only in a written report. Verbally notify the relevant person on the installation — installation manager, area authority, or operator representative — before leaving the area, and record that you did so in the report: "Safety-critical finding verbally notified to [Name, Title] at [time] on [date]."

Photographic evidence for offshore inspections

Photography on offshore platforms often requires a hot work permit or intrinsically safe camera in hazardous areas. Confirm the photography rules with the operator's PTW system before starting. Once cleared:

  • Photograph the equipment nameplate and tag number for every major item inspected
  • Photograph the overall equipment and its installation context before focusing on defects
  • For corrosion findings, photograph the extent — not just the worst spot
  • Include a scale reference (ruler, coin, or known object) for size-critical findings
  • Caption every photo immediately — "Nameplate, HP separator V-301" and "Corrosion on north nozzle flange, V-301, depth approx. 3mm" are complete captions

Offline reporting on offshore facilities

Satellite internet on offshore platforms is typically restricted to operational communications. Cellular coverage is often absent entirely. Your reporting workflow must function without connectivity:

  • All photos saved locally on the device as you take them
  • Notes and observations captured offline
  • Report draft generated on the device
  • Report shared via email or satellite data transfer when connectivity allows

Frequently asked questions

What standards apply to offshore inspection reports?

The applicable standards depend on the inspection type, location, and operator requirements. Common references include API 510 (pressure vessels), API 570 (piping), API 653 (storage tanks), ISO 19901 series (offshore structures), and NACE/AMPP standards for corrosion assessment. Always confirm the applicable codes and standards with the client or operator before commencing the inspection.

How quickly should an offshore inspection report be delivered?

For routine inspections, within 24–48 hours of leaving the installation is the professional standard. For safety-critical or urgent findings, a preliminary written report should be issued within hours — certainly before the end of the same day. Many operator contracts specify maximum delivery times for different report types.
