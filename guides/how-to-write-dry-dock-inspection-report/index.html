How to Write a Dry Dock Inspection Report

For marine surveyors, ship managers, and dry dock superintendents  ·  Updated May 2026

A dry dock inspection report documents the condition of a vessel's hull, underwater equipment, and selected systems during a dry-docking period. It serves as the record for the ship owner, manager, class society, and insurer, and forms the basis for repair specification and cost allocation. A well-structured dry dock report provides a clear before/after record and a defensible account of what was found, what was done, and what was accepted.

Types of dry dock inspection report

  • Initial hull condition report — produced immediately after undocking, before any cleaning or repairs, documenting as-found hull condition
  • Repair progress report — issued at key stages during the dry-dock period to update the owner/manager on repair progress
  • Class survey report — issued by the class surveyor following statutory surveys conducted during dry dock (typically for the five-year special survey or intermediate docking)
  • Owner's representative report — produced by the superintendent or attending surveyor representing the owner, summarising all observations and repair outcomes
  • Final completion report — documents all repairs completed, outstanding items, and vessel condition at undocking

Standard structure of a dry dock inspection report

  1. Vessel particulars — name, IMO, flag, class, owner, manager, yard, dock entry/exit dates
  2. Inspection scope and attendees
  3. Hull condition as found — plating condition, corrosion mapping, paint system assessment
  4. Underwater equipment — propeller, rudder, sea chests, anodes, stern tube, bow thruster
  5. Structural findings — any deformation, damage, or class-required thickness measurements
  6. Repairs carried out — detailed record of all work completed, specifications, and materials
  7. Outstanding items — work deferred, class conditions, or owner's outstanding items
  8. Photo appendix — systematic coverage of hull condition and key repair items
  9. Recommendations
  10. Signature block

Hull condition documentation

The hull condition section is the most detailed part of a dry dock report. Document systematically — port side forward to aft, then starboard side, then bottom. For each shell plate or zone, record:

  • Plate condition: good, fair, pitted, or heavily corroded
  • Coating condition: intact, spotted, breakdown percentage, any bare steel
  • Thickness measurements where required by class or where condition warrants
  • Any deformation, impact damage, or repair welds visible
  • Anode coverage and condition

Document before cleaning, not after: The as-found hull condition should be photographed and recorded before high-pressure washing and blasting. Once the hull is cleaned, the original corrosion extent and coating condition cannot be recovered. The pre-cleaning record is often the most important evidence in a dry dock report, particularly for insurance claims or disputes with the yard about additional work scope.

Underwater equipment

For each major underwater component, record:

  • Propeller — blade condition, edge damage, erosion, pitch measurement if required by class
  • Rudder — condition of plating, pintles, gudgeons, sacrificial anodes, any cavitation damage
  • Stern tube — seal condition, bearing clearances, oil/water sealing type, any signs of previous leakage
  • Sea chests — grids condition, internal fouling, valve condition
  • Sacrificial anodes — percentage wastage, coverage, type
  • Bow thruster — tunnel condition, propeller condition, seal condition

Photography in dry dock

A dry dock inspection should produce a comprehensive photographic record. Minimum coverage:

  • Hull overview shots — both sides, bow, stern, and bottom — before and after cleaning
  • Any significant corrosion or damage found during pre-cleaning inspection
  • All major repairs in progress and on completion
  • Propeller and rudder before and after any work
  • Anode replacement — before removal and after fitting
  • Class survey items — as required by the attending surveyor

Caption every photo with location, item, and what is being shown. For hull photos, a grid reference system (Frames and waterlines) makes location precise and reproducible across reports.

Daily progress reports

For dry-dockings lasting more than 2–3 days, issue a daily progress report to the owner and manager. Keep it brief: work completed today, work planned for tomorrow, any issues or delays, and any additional findings requiring a decision. The daily reports build a contemporaneous record that supports the final report.

Frequently asked questions

Who attends a dry dock inspection and who writes the report?

The owner's superintendent or technical manager typically attends throughout the dry-docking. The class surveyor attends for statutory surveys. An independent marine surveyor may attend on behalf of the insurer (P&I or H&M). Each attending party may produce their own report. The owner's superintendent produces the owner's representative report, which is the most comprehensive — covering all observations, all repairs, and all decisions made during the dry-docking.

What class documentation is required as part of a dry dock?

Requirements vary by class society and survey type. For a five-year special survey, class typically requires thickness measurements of specified hull areas, close-up surveys of specific structural items, and survey of all main machinery and safety systems. The class surveyor issues a survey report and updates the vessel's class record. The owner's superintendent report should be aligned with — but is separate from — the class survey documentation.
