How to Write an MEP Inspection Report

For HVAC engineers, MEP technicians, AMC teams, and facilities managers  ·  Updated May 2026

An MEP inspection report documents the condition, performance, and compliance status of a building's mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems following an inspection or service visit. For AMC (Annual Maintenance Contract) teams, it is the primary deliverable that proves the visit happened, records what was found, and protects the company from liability when something fails.

This guide covers what should go in an MEP inspection report, how to structure it by system, how to capture evidence efficiently on site, and how to speed up report delivery without compromising quality.

What is an MEP inspection report?

An MEP inspection report is a structured document produced by a qualified engineer or technician after inspecting or servicing one or more building systems. It records the condition of each system, any defects or non-conformances found, remedial work carried out, and any outstanding actions required.

Common MEP inspection report types include:

  • HVAC preventive maintenance report
  • Electrical inspection and testing report
  • Plumbing and drainage inspection report
  • Fire alarm and suppression system inspection report
  • Elevator and escalator inspection report
  • AMC periodic service report (monthly, quarterly, annual)
  • Handover or commissioning inspection report
  • Defect liability period inspection report

Writing findings by MEP system

Mechanical — HVAC and ventilation

For each air handling unit, fan coil unit, chiller, cooling tower, or ventilation system inspected, record:

  • Unit tag number or location reference
  • Overall condition (good / fair / requires attention / defective)
  • Filter condition and whether replaced or cleaned
  • Belt condition (tension, wear)
  • Coil condition (fouling, fin damage)
  • Refrigerant observations (no leaks visible / leak suspected / leak confirmed)
  • Drain pan and condensate drain condition
  • Supply and return air temperatures if measured
  • Noise or vibration observations
  • Any alarms active on BMS or local control panel

Electrical systems

For electrical inspections, record:

  • Panel or DB reference and location
  • Visual condition of panel, connections, and labelling
  • Any tripped breakers, loose connections, or signs of overheating
  • Thermographic observations if applicable
  • Earth continuity and insulation resistance test results (if testing conducted)
  • Emergency lighting and exit sign operation
  • UPS condition and battery state
  • Generator test run results and fuel level

On electrical test reports: Formal electrical installation condition reports (EICRs) require specific test instruments, qualifications, and documentation formats. A routine AMC visual inspection report is not the same as a statutory electrical test report. Be clear in your report which type of inspection you conducted.

Plumbing and drainage

For plumbing inspections, record:

  • Water supply pressure at point of inspection
  • Booster pump condition and pressure readings
  • Water tank condition (cleanliness, level, float valve operation)
  • Hot water system temperature and any legionella risk observations
  • Visible pipework condition (corrosion, insulation, supports)
  • Drain flow observations (slow drainage, blockages, odours)
  • Sanitary ware condition
  • Any active leaks observed and their location

Recording readings and measurements

Numerical readings give your report objective weight. Whenever you take a measurement, record:

  • The exact reading with units (°C not just "temperature", kPa not just "pressure")
  • The acceptable range or design specification for that parameter
  • Whether the reading is within or outside the acceptable range
  • The instrument used (for formal inspection reports)

Readings without context are nearly useless. "Supply air temperature: 14°C" means nothing without knowing the design setpoint. "Supply air temperature: 14°C (design: 12–16°C — within range)" is a professional observation.

The AMC reporting problem

AMC teams visit multiple sites per day. The commercial pressure to complete visits quickly means reports are often written at the end of the day from memory, or not written at all until a client complaint forces the issue. This creates serious liability exposure — if a system fails and there is no contemporaneous inspection record, the AMC company has no defence.

The answer is to capture evidence during the visit, not after it. A photo taken and captioned at the time of inspection — "Chiller 1, compressor discharge pressure 18 bar — within range" — takes fifteen seconds. Reconstructing that observation from memory at 9pm takes five minutes and is less accurate.

AMC best practice: Treat the report as part of the visit, not as admin to be done afterwards. Every system you inspect should have at least one photo record in your report by the time you move to the next system.

Common mistakes in MEP inspection reports

  • No readings recorded — observations without measurements are difficult to defend or trend over time
  • Generic findings — "system in good condition" with no supporting evidence is not useful. What specifically did you inspect and what specifically did you find?
  • Missing scope statement — if you only inspected the HVAC and not the electrical, state this clearly
  • No photo evidence — especially for defects. A defect without a photo is your word against the client's
  • Outstanding items not tracked — defects noted in a previous report that are still unresolved should appear as carry-forward items with their original date
  • No client acknowledgement — for AMC reports, a client countersignature or email acknowledgement confirms the visit took place and the findings were communicated

MEP inspection report template (section headings)

  1. Report reference, date, and visit type
  2. Prepared by (name, qualifications, company)
  3. Client and building details
  4. Contract reference and visit frequency
  5. Scope of inspection
  6. Mechanical findings (HVAC, ventilation, chillers)
  7. Electrical findings (panels, lighting, emergency systems, generators)
  8. Plumbing findings (water supply, drainage, sanitary)
  9. Readings and measurements log
  10. Work carried out during this visit
  11. Photo appendix
  12. Outstanding items (carried forward)
  13. Action items (action, responsible party, target date)
  14. Technician signature and client countersignature

Frequently asked questions

How detailed should an AMC inspection report be?

Detailed enough to demonstrate that the inspection was genuinely conducted and that findings are specific to this visit at this site. Generic boilerplate that could apply to any building is worse than useless — it suggests the visit may not have taken place at all. System-specific observations with readings and at least one photo per major item is the professional standard.

Should the client sign the MEP inspection report?

Best practice is to obtain a client signature or written acknowledgement (email is acceptable) confirming the visit took place and the report was received. This is especially important for AMC contracts where billing is tied to completed visits. Without acknowledgement, the client can later dispute whether the visit occurred.

How long should I retain MEP inspection reports?

Retain for at least the duration of the AMC contract plus the applicable limitation period for professional negligence claims in your jurisdiction — typically six years minimum. For systems with safety implications (fire suppression, electrical), longer retention is advisable. Check your insurance requirements.
