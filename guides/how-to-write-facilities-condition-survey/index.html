Facilities Management Guide

How to Write a Facilities Condition Survey

For facilities managers, building surveyors, and asset managers  ·  Updated May 2026

A facilities condition survey (FCS) — also called a building condition survey or asset condition assessment — provides a systematic record of the physical state of a building, its systems, and its assets. It is the foundation for maintenance planning, capital expenditure budgeting, lease negotiations, and acquisition due diligence. A well-executed FCS gives decision-makers the data they need to prioritise spending and manage risk over a 5–10 year horizon.

Building element framework

Structure your condition findings using a standard element breakdown. A common framework adapted from RICS or BSRIA standards:

  • Structure — foundations, frame, floor structure, roof structure
  • Envelope — roof covering, external walls, windows, doors, cladding
  • Internal fabric — internal walls and partitions, ceilings, floors, internal doors
  • Mechanical — HVAC systems, plumbing, drainage, lifts/escalators
  • Electrical — distribution, lighting, power, data infrastructure, UPS
  • External works — car parking, landscaping, drainage, boundary structures
  • Safety systems — fire detection, suppression, emergency lighting, access control

Condition rating scale

Apply a consistent condition rating to each element. A standard five-point scale:

GradeConditionAction required
AAs new or recently refurbished. No defects.Normal planned maintenance only.
BSound. Minor wear or deterioration. Performing adequately.Routine maintenance. Monitor.
COperational but showing significant wear. Repair required within 1–3 years.Plan and budget for repair.
DSignificant deterioration. Performance impaired. Repair or replacement needed.Prioritise within 12 months.
EFailed or at end of life. Significant risk or non-compliance.Immediate action required.

Consistency is everything: Apply the same rating scale with the same definitions throughout. A Grade C on the roof structure should mean the same thing as a Grade C on the HVAC system. Inconsistent grading undermines the cost plan and the overall condition score.

The cost plan

The cost plan is often the most valuable output of an FCS. It translates condition findings into maintenance expenditure forecasts, typically over 5 or 10 years. Structure it as follows:

  • Year 1 (immediate) — Grade D and E items, safety-critical repairs
  • Years 1–3 (short-term) — Grade C items approaching failure, planned replacement of end-of-life assets
  • Years 3–5 (medium-term) — Lifecycle replacements based on asset age and condition
  • Years 5–10 (long-term) — Major envelope and structure works, system overhauls

Use elemental cost rates from recognised sources (RICS Building Maintenance Information, BCIS, or your own schedule of rates). Be clear that all cost estimates are indicative and subject to detailed design and tender.

Photography in a facilities condition survey

Photos are the evidence that supports your condition ratings. For a large building survey, you may capture 100–300 photos. Manage them systematically:

  • Caption each photo immediately on site — location, element, and what the photo shows
  • Photograph both good and poor condition — a photo of Grade A roofing is baseline evidence, not just a record of defects
  • Include a wide shot and a close-up for significant deficiencies
  • Photograph nameplates and data labels on plant and equipment for the asset register

Common mistakes in facilities condition surveys

  • Treating it as a defect list only — a condition survey is a condition profile. Grade A and B elements should be recorded as clearly as D and E items. A report that only records problems gives a distorted picture.
  • Missing the asset register — the asset register is what makes the cost plan defensible. Without an itemised asset list with ages and remaining lives, the 5-year cost forecast is guesswork.
  • Vague cost estimates — "allow for roof repairs" is not a cost plan line. "Allow for replacement of approximately 450m² flat roof covering, existing felt to be stripped and replaced with single-ply membrane: estimated cost £XX,XXX" is.
  • No scope limitations — if areas were locked, occupied, or excluded, state this. An unqualified survey implies full access.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a facilities condition survey be carried out?

For most commercial and institutional buildings, a full FCS every 3–5 years is standard practice, with annual update inspections between full surveys. For buildings with significant capital expenditure programmes or acquisition/disposal activity, more frequent surveys may be warranted.

What is the difference between a facilities condition survey and a building survey?

A building survey (or structural survey) focuses primarily on defects in the fabric and structure. A facilities condition survey is broader — it covers all building systems and assets, produces an asset register, and generates a long-term maintenance cost plan. An FCS is primarily a management and planning tool; a building survey is primarily a technical assessment for a specific purpose such as purchase or insurance.

Can the cost plan from an FCS be used for capital budgeting?

Yes — this is one of its primary uses. The cost plan from an FCS provides facilities and finance teams with a multi-year maintenance expenditure forecast that can be used to support budget submissions, capital planning, and life-cycle cost modelling. All cost plan estimates should be clearly identified as indicative and reviewed before committing to procurement.
