How to Write a Fire Safety Inspection Report

For fire risk assessors, safety officers, and compliance inspectors  ·  Updated May 2026

A fire safety inspection report documents the fire safety compliance status of a building, facility, or vessel. It identifies deficiencies, quantifies risk, and provides actionable recommendations that building owners and managers can act on. A well-written fire safety report is clear, evidence-backed, and specific — vague findings help no one and create liability for the assessor.

What a fire safety inspection report must include

SectionWhat it contains
Property detailsAddress, building type, occupancy class, number of storeys, approximate floor area
Inspection detailsDate, inspector name and qualifications, scope of inspection
Building overviewConstruction type, occupancy, relevant history, previous reports if available
Findings — by areaFire safety conditions observed in each zone, floor, or area inspected
Non-compliance listNumbered list of deficiencies with severity, location, and required action
Risk ratingOverall fire risk level for the premises (e.g. Low / Medium / High / Critical)
Photo appendixNumbered, captioned photographs of findings and deficiencies
RecommendationsSpecific remedial actions with priority and suggested timeframe

Structuring your findings

The most effective fire safety reports organise findings by building area or system, not by severity. Walk through each area in logical sequence — ground floor, then upper floors, then common areas, roof, plant rooms — and document what you find. Then compile the non-compliance list separately, ranked by severity.

Non-compliance severity ratings

Use a consistent severity scale throughout. A common four-level system:

  • Critical — Immediate risk to life. Requires immediate action. Examples: blocked fire escape routes, non-functional fire suppression systems, missing fire doors on critical escape routes.
  • High — Significant risk. Action required within 28 days. Examples: defective emergency lighting, inadequate fire compartmentation, missing signage on escape routes.
  • Medium — Moderate risk. Action required within 90 days. Examples: fire extinguishers out of service date, minor compartmentation breaches, missing maintenance records.
  • Low — Minor deficiency. Action recommended at next planned maintenance. Examples: minor signage issues, documentation gaps, housekeeping observations.

One finding per item: Never combine multiple deficiencies into a single finding. "Fire door on third floor corridor has damaged intumescent strip, missing closer, and is being propped open" is three separate findings, each with its own severity and recommended action. Combining them makes it harder to track remediation and easier for building managers to partially address and consider the item closed.

What to photograph in a fire safety inspection

Photographic evidence is the most defensible part of a fire safety report. For each deficiency, capture:

  • Orientation shot — shows the location in context. "Third floor corridor looking south" is a useful orientation. A close-up alone without context is not.
  • Detail shot — the specific deficiency clearly visible. Close enough to see the damage, obstruction, or non-compliant condition.
  • Identifier in frame — where possible, include a reference point such as a door number, floor level sign, or zone label.

Caption every photo immediately on site: "Fire door 3F-07 — intumescent strip missing from door edge, hinge side" is a complete caption. A photo with no caption is of limited evidential value if the report is ever challenged.

Common weaknesses in fire safety reports

  • Vague finding descriptions — "Fire door not compliant" tells the building manager nothing. "Fire door on Level 2, Stairwell B has a defective self-closing mechanism that does not fully close the door to the frame — gap of approximately 15mm remains when released" is actionable.
  • Missing locations — every finding must have a precise location. Floor, zone, door number, or grid reference. Without it, the building manager cannot find the deficiency to fix it.
  • No timeframes on recommendations — "Remediate as soon as possible" is not a recommendation. "Replace defective self-closer within 28 days" is.
  • Insufficient photo evidence — if your finding is challenged, photographs are your defence. One photo per finding is a minimum. Two (orientation and detail) is professional standard.
  • Missing scope limitations — if areas were inaccessible or excluded from scope, state this explicitly. An unreported area implies it was inspected.

Fire safety inspection areas to cover

  • Means of escape — all exit routes, stairwells, corridors, and assembly points
  • Fire doors — condition, closers, intumescent strips, signage, propping
  • Emergency lighting — coverage, condition, test records
  • Fire detection and alarm systems — coverage, condition, last service date
  • Fire suppression systems — sprinklers, suppression heads, control panels
  • Fire compartmentation — walls, floors, penetrations, service routes
  • Portable fire fighting equipment — extinguisher type, location, service dates
  • Signage — exit signs, assembly points, equipment locations
  • Fire safety management records — training, drills, maintenance logs
  • Storage and housekeeping — flammable materials, obstruction of escape routes

Frequently asked questions

What qualifications are needed to write a fire safety inspection report?

Requirements vary by jurisdiction and purpose. In the UK, a fire risk assessment for most premises must be carried out by a competent person — typically a qualified fire risk assessor (e.g. IFE, IFSM, or equivalent). In other regions, fire safety inspections may be conducted by licensed inspectors, fire safety officers, or building control professionals. Always confirm the regulatory requirements for the specific jurisdiction and premises type before undertaking an inspection.

How long should a fire safety inspection report be retained?

Retain fire safety inspection reports for the life of the building plus a reasonable period after. In regulated premises, minimum retention periods are typically 5–10 years. In practice, retaining all records indefinitely (in digital form) is the safest approach, as fire incidents can give rise to legal proceedings many years after the event.
