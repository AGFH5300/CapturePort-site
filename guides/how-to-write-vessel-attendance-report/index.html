How to Write a Vessel Attendance Report

For marine engineers, service companies, attendance officers, and ship managers  ·  Updated May 2026

A vessel attendance report is the standard record of a service visit, inspection, or survey attendance on board a vessel. It confirms that the attendance took place, documents what was found and what was done, and forms the basis for billing, maintenance records, and client communication. A clear, prompt attendance report is a professional obligation and a commercial necessity.

What is a vessel attendance report?

A vessel attendance report (sometimes called a ship attendance report or vessel visit report) documents a specific visit to a vessel by a marine professional — engineer, surveyor, superintendent, or service technician. It is distinct from a full survey report in that it focuses on the specific scope of the attendance rather than a comprehensive vessel assessment.

Attendance reports are used by:

  • Marine service companies billing for attendance time and work
  • Ship managers tracking maintenance and service history
  • Marine surveyors documenting attendance scope and findings
  • Port captains and company attendance officers recording visits
  • P&I clubs and insurers tracking claims-related attendances

Key sections in detail

Attendance details — precision matters

Record the exact port and berth or anchorage position. Record the precise time you boarded and the time you departed. This is not bureaucracy — it is the basis for billing, and it may be required if the attendance is later referenced in a claim, dispute, or class record. Include the vessel's port state at the time: "Vessel berthed at Jebel Ali Port, Berth 12, awaiting cargo operations."

Purpose of attendance

State the reason for the attendance exactly as instructed. If the instruction was "attend to investigate main engine vibration", write that. If additional scope was agreed on board, note what was added and by whose authority: "Additional scope agreed with Chief Engineer on board to include inspection of turbocharger bearings."

Scope versus findings

The scope is what you were asked to do and what you did. The findings are what you observed. Keep these distinct. "Inspected main engine — found excessive play in connecting rod bearing No. 3" is correct. Mixing scope and findings into a single narrative makes the report harder to read and harder to reference later.

The countersignature: Always aim to obtain a signature from the Chief Engineer or senior officer on board confirming the attendance took place and the findings were communicated. This eliminates disputes about whether the visit occurred and prevents later claims that findings were not notified. If the officer declines to sign, note this in your report.

Time record

Record time boarded, time departed, and any significant interruptions. For billing purposes, also note travel time if that is included in your agreement. If the attendance extended beyond normal working hours, note the start of overtime. Time disputes are common in marine service — a clear time record in the report prevents them.

Vessel attendance report delivery standards

Ship managers and operators make operational decisions based on attendance reports. Delivery timelines matter:

  • Routine service and maintenance attendance: Same day or by the following morning
  • Emergency or casualty attendance: Preliminary report within 2–4 hours; full report within 24 hours
  • Survey or inspection attendance: Within 24–48 hours unless a longer timeline is agreed in advance

A report delivered three days after the attendance raises questions about the quality and contemporaneity of the record. Always agree the expected delivery time with the instructing party before attending.

Vessel attendance report for multi-day jobs

For attendances spanning multiple days — dry-dock work, extensive repairs, complex surveys — issue a daily progress report in addition to the final attendance report. Daily reports do not need to be long: vessel details, date, work carried out today, work planned for tomorrow, any issues encountered. The daily report keeps the client informed and builds a contemporaneous record that supports the final report.

Frequently asked questions

How is a vessel attendance report different from a vessel inspection report?

A vessel attendance report focuses on a specific visit — what was done during that attendance, when, and by whom. A vessel inspection report typically covers a broader condition assessment. In practice, many attendance reports contain both — a record of the work done and a condition assessment of the inspected equipment. The key difference is scope: attendance reports are visit-specific; inspection reports may cover the whole vessel or a system comprehensively.

Is a vessel attendance report required for billing?

In most marine service contracts, yes. The attendance report (or a summary of it) forms the basis for the service invoice. Without a signed attendance report, disputes about whether work was carried out, how long it took, and what scope was covered are much harder to resolve. Treat the attendance report as a commercial document, not just a technical one.

What if the vessel was inaccessible or the attendance was brief?

Always produce a report, even for brief or unsuccessful attendances. "Attended vessel at 09:30. Access to engine room refused by vessel Master pending owner's instructions. Departed at 10:15. Awaiting re-instruction from ship manager." That is a complete and useful attendance report. It documents the professional's attendance and the circumstances — which may be important later.
