How to Write a Marine Service Report

For marine service engineers, repair teams, and attendance officers  ·  Updated May 2026

A marine service report is the formal record of a service visit, repair attendance, or maintenance job on a vessel or marine facility. It documents what condition was found, what work was carried out, what parts were used, and what remains outstanding. A well-written service report protects the service company, satisfies the client, and creates a clear maintenance history for the vessel.

What is a marine service report?

A marine service report (also called a vessel service report or ship repair report) is produced by a marine engineer, technician, or service company representative following a job on board a vessel or at a marine facility. Unlike a survey report, the primary focus is on work performed rather than condition assessment alone — though condition documentation before and after work is equally important.

Common marine service report types include:

  • Main engine service report (overhaul, running maintenance, fuel system work)
  • Auxiliary machinery service report (generators, pumps, compressors, separators)
  • Electrical system service report
  • Cargo gear and crane service report
  • Navigation and communication equipment service report
  • Hydraulic system service and repair report
  • Propulsion and steering system report
  • Safety equipment inspection and service report

Section by section: what to write

Vessel particulars and attendance details

Always confirm the vessel name, IMO number, and flag before starting the report. An incorrect IMO number on a service report creates billing, warranty, and class record problems. Note the port and exact date of attendance. Record who instructed the job — ship manager, owner, P&I club — and any relevant job reference or purchase order number.

Scope of work

Describe the instructed scope precisely. Then note any deviation from the original instruction — additional work found necessary, work that could not be completed, or scope limitations. If the original instruction was "inspect and repair the main engine fuel pump" but you found three pumps requiring attention, record this clearly.

Condition found

This is the baseline. Document the condition of the equipment before any work was done. This protects you if a dispute arises about whether a pre-existing problem was caused by your work. Include:

  • Running hours if displayed on the equipment
  • Visible wear, damage, or deterioration before work started
  • Any active faults, alarms, or error codes
  • Performance data if measurable (pressures, temperatures, outputs)

Work carried out

This is the core of the service report. Be specific. "Overhauled fuel pump" is not acceptable. "Disassembled fuel pump unit 1, replaced barrel and plunger assembly (OEM part ref. [X]), replaced O-ring set, reassembled, bench-tested, installed and function-tested at sea pressure" is a professional record.

Work should be recorded in chronological order. For multi-day attendances, organise by date.

Part number discipline: Always record OEM part numbers and quantities for every part replaced. This is essential for warranty claims, class records, and future maintenance planning. A service report without part references is significantly less valuable to the ship manager and vessel owner.

Photo appendix

Photos are essential evidence in any service report. For service work specifically:

  • Photograph the condition found before work commences
  • Photograph the removed components — worn parts, damaged components
  • Photograph work in progress for complex jobs
  • Photograph the completed installation
  • Caption every photo immediately on site: "Main engine HP fuel pump — worn plunger before replacement" is useful. "IMG_3847.jpg" is not.

Outstanding items and recommendations

Be explicit about what was not completed and why. "Unable to replace shaft seal due to absence of vessel-specific spare — recommend replacement at next port call with part ref [X] on board" is a professional record. Never omit outstanding items to make the report look cleaner — this creates liability.

Capturing service evidence efficiently on board

Marine service engineers typically carry out the job, take photos, and then try to write the report back at the office or hotel — often 4–6 hours after leaving the vessel. By then, part numbers are unclear, photo context is forgotten, and the sequence of work is hazy.

The better workflow is to treat report capture as part of the job itself: photograph before you start, note the condition, photograph key steps, note the work as you complete each phase. By the time you step off the gangway, the report is 70% written.

Frequently asked questions

Should a marine service report be signed by the Chief Engineer?

Best practice is to obtain a countersignature from the vessel's Chief Engineer (or senior officer in their absence). This confirms the work was carried out as described, the vessel's personnel were informed, and any outstanding items were communicated. Without a countersignature, the vessel's management can later dispute the scope or outcome of the work.

How soon should a marine service report be delivered?

For routine service attendances, the same day or by the following morning is the professional standard. Ship managers make operational decisions based on your report — a report delivered two days later has diminished value and may create trust issues.

What if the work could not be completed?

Record it explicitly. State what was attempted, what prevented completion (missing parts, vessel constraint, time limitation, safety concern), and what is recommended next. An honest incomplete report is far more professional than a vague report that obscures what was not done.
