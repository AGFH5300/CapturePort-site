How to Write a Vessel Inspection Report

For marine engineers, surveyors, attendance officers, and service teams  ·  Updated May 2026

A vessel inspection report is the formal record of a marine attendance, service visit, or survey. It documents what was found, what was done, what remains outstanding, and what is recommended — and it protects everyone involved. A poor report creates liability. A clear, photo-backed report builds client trust and professional credibility.

This guide covers exactly what should go into a vessel inspection report, how to structure it, how to capture effective evidence on board, and how modern mobile tools eliminate the common problem of writing up reports hours after leaving the vessel.

What is a vessel inspection report?

A vessel inspection report is an official written record created by a qualified marine professional — an engineer, surveyor, attendance officer, or service technician — after attending a vessel for inspection, maintenance, repair, survey, or emergency response purposes.

These reports are used by ship owners, ship managers, P&I clubs, flag states, port state control authorities, class societies, insurance underwriters, and loss adjusters. Depending on the nature of the inspection, a report may be contractually required, class-mandated, or simply good professional practice.

Common types include:

  • Vessel attendance report (service or repair visit)
  • Condition survey report
  • Damage inspection report
  • Pre-purchase survey report
  • Dry-dock progress report
  • Equipment inspection report (main engine, generators, cargo gear)
  • Hull and structural inspection report
  • Insurance inspection or loss adjustment report

Standard structure of a vessel inspection report

Regardless of report type, professional vessel inspection reports follow a consistent structure. Deviating from this without reason creates confusion and suggests lack of experience.

Section by section: what to write

Vessel and job details

Collect this information before you step on board. Vessel name, IMO number, flag, port of inspection, date and time of attendance, your name and qualifications, the instructing party, and the job reference number. These details must be accurate — an incorrect IMO number on an insurance report creates serious problems.

Scope of inspection

Be explicit about what you were asked to inspect and what you were not. If you attended to inspect the main engine and observed no other machinery, say so. If access to the cargo hold was restricted, record it. This protects you and sets accurate expectations for the reader.

Best practice: Always state whether your inspection was visual only, operational (equipment was run), or intrusive (covers removed, measurements taken). This is the single most misunderstood section in marine reports and the source of most professional disputes.

Findings and observations

Work systematically through each inspected area. For each finding, record:

  • The location or component (use standard nautical terminology — frame numbers, deck levels, port/starboard)
  • The condition found (good, fair, poor, defective)
  • Specific observations (corrosion extent, crack dimensions, oil leaks, operational performance)
  • Work carried out (if a service visit)
  • Photo reference numbers for supporting evidence

Write in plain, factual language. Avoid speculation. If you cannot determine the cause of a defect within the scope of your attendance, say so explicitly.

Photo appendix

Photos are the most important evidence in any vessel inspection report. A written finding without photographic support is easy to challenge. Follow these rules:

  • Caption every photo at the time of capture — not hours later from memory
  • Include orientation shots (wide view) before close-up detail shots
  • Number photos sequentially and reference them in the findings section
  • Ensure photos are in focus, adequately lit, and show the defect or condition clearly
  • Include a scale reference (ruler, hand, or known object) for dimension-critical findings

Recommendations

Recommendations must be clear and actionable. Vague recommendations ("repairs should be considered") are unhelpful and may create liability. State specifically what should be done, by whom (class-approved contractor, OEM, etc.), and if possible, by when.

Signature and qualifications

Always include your full name, professional qualifications (CoC grade, IIMS/IMarEST membership, surveyor accreditation), your company's name, and the date of signature. Some reports require countersigning by a senior surveyor or class representative.

How to capture evidence effectively on board

The most common failure in vessel inspection reporting is not the writing — it is the evidence capture. Most professionals leave the vessel with a phone full of uncaptioned photos, voice memos they cannot find, and paper notes they cannot read. The report then gets written from memory, often incomplete.

Effective on-board evidence capture means:

  • Captioning photos immediately after taking them, before moving to the next area
  • Recording voice observations when your hands are occupied or conditions make typing difficult
  • Noting location references in captions (frame 42, starboard side, engine room bilge)
  • Capturing orientation and context shots alongside close-up detail shots
  • Working offline — vessels frequently have no connectivity in engine rooms, cargo holds, or at anchorage

On connectivity: Marine inspections frequently take place in locations with no mobile data coverage — deep in an engine room, at anchorage, or in a remote port. Any reporting tool you use must work completely offline. Cloud-dependent tools that require a connection to save your work are not suitable for marine field use.

Common mistakes in vessel inspection reports

  • Writing the report from memory — always capture observations in the field. Memory degrades rapidly after leaving the vessel.
  • Uncaptioned or unordered photos — a photo appendix with 40 unnumbered images and no captions is nearly useless as evidence.
  • Vague scope statements — "inspected vessel" is not a scope. Be specific about what you inspected and what was excluded.
  • Speculative language — "this appears to have been caused by..." Report what you observed. Leave causation to experts who have conducted a full investigation.
  • Missing qualifications — your report is only as credible as your stated qualifications. Always include them.
  • Late delivery — a report delivered three days after the attendance loses credibility. Report promptly.

Vessel inspection report template (section headings)

Use this as a checklist when structuring any vessel inspection report:

  1. Report reference and date
  2. Prepared by (name, qualifications, company)
  3. Vessel particulars (name, IMO, flag, type, class, GRT, year built)
  4. Attendance details (port, location, date, instructing party, job reference)
  5. Scope of inspection
  6. Findings — area by area or system by system
  7. Work carried out (for service/repair attendances)
  8. Photo appendix (numbered, captioned)
  9. Recommendations
  10. Remarks and limitations
  11. Signature and date

Frequently asked questions

How long should a vessel inspection report be?

Long enough to be complete and credible, short enough to be read. A routine service attendance might produce a 2–4 page report. A complex damage survey might run to 20+ pages with a substantial photo appendix. Length should be driven by the findings, not by a page target.

What format should a vessel inspection report be in?

PDF is the standard for final delivery — it is tamper-evident and consistent across devices. Word format is acceptable for reports that need to be reviewed or amended by the client before finalisation. Always keep a signed PDF as your official record.

Do I need to be a licensed surveyor to write a vessel inspection report?

That depends on the purpose of the report. Service attendance reports do not require a licensed surveyor. Insurance, class, statutory, and flag state reports typically require specific qualifications or authority. Check the requirements of the instructing party before attending.

How quickly should a vessel inspection report be delivered?

For routine service attendances, same-day or next-day is the professional standard. For complex surveys, within 48–72 hours. Insurance and legal matters may have specific deadlines specified in the instruction. Always confirm the expected delivery date with the instructing party before attending.
