How to Write an Equipment Inspection Report

For maintenance engineers, field service teams, and equipment inspectors  ·  Updated May 2026

An equipment inspection report documents the condition, performance, and maintenance status of a piece of machinery, plant, or technical equipment following a physical inspection. It is the record that drives maintenance decisions, justifies repair or replacement costs, and provides the evidence base for warranty claims, insurance assessments, and regulatory compliance.

This guide covers how to write a complete, professional equipment inspection report — from preparation to delivery.

When is an equipment inspection report required?

  • Scheduled preventive maintenance inspections
  • Pre-purchase or pre-lease condition assessment
  • Post-incident or failure investigation
  • Insurance or warranty claim support
  • Statutory or regulatory compliance inspection
  • End-of-lease condition report
  • Decommissioning or disposal assessment
  • Handover between operators or owners

Equipment identification — get it right first

Record the equipment details precisely before beginning the inspection. Make, model, serial number, and asset tag are the minimum. Also record:

  • Installation date or estimated age if installation date is unknown
  • Current running hours from the hour meter (photograph the meter)
  • Physical location — building, floor, zone, room, or plant designation
  • Last service date and service company from the service record or log

An equipment inspection report tied to the correct serial number is verifiable. One without correct identification is of limited value if the equipment is ever subject to a warranty claim, insurance assessment, or regulatory audit.

Condition ratings

Use a consistent, defined condition rating scale throughout your report. Never use subjective terms like "OK" or "not bad" in a professional inspection report. Here is a standard five-point scale:

RatingDefinitionAction required
1 — GoodEquipment in sound working condition. No defects observed.Continue normal scheduled maintenance.
2 — FairMinor wear or deterioration observed. Functional but approaching service interval.Monitor. Service at next scheduled interval.
3 — Requires attentionDefects or wear that require correction within the next service cycle.Plan maintenance within [timeframe].
4 — PoorSignificant defects affecting performance or reliability. Continued operation at risk.Immediate maintenance required.
5 — Failed / unserviceableEquipment failed or unsafe to operate.Remove from service. Repair or replace before returning to operation.

Apply a condition rating to each major component you inspect, and then an overall rating to the equipment as a whole. The overall rating should reflect the worst significant defect found — a pump with one seized bearing is rated Poor overall, not Fair because the other components are acceptable.

Inspection findings — component by component

Work through each major component or system in a consistent order. For each, record:

  • Condition rating (from the scale above)
  • Specific observations — what you saw, heard, measured, or tested
  • Running hours if displayed on the component
  • Evidence of wear patterns, contamination, overheating, vibration, or damage
  • Comparison to previous inspection if records are available
  • Photo reference number for supporting photos

Inspect in a consistent order every time. For repetitive equipment types (generators, pumps, compressors), always follow the same component sequence. This prevents missed items, makes reports comparable across inspections, and allows trends to be tracked over time.

Measurements and test results

Where your inspection includes measurements or test results, record:

  • The exact value with units
  • The acceptable range or manufacturer specification
  • Whether the reading is within or outside specification
  • The instrument used for critical measurements

Common measurements for equipment inspections include: oil analysis results, vibration amplitude and frequency, electrical insulation resistance, motor winding resistance, bearing clearances, pressure test results, flow rates, and operating temperatures.

Defect list

Compile a numbered defect list from your component-by-component findings. Each defect entry should include: defect number, location/component, description, severity rating, and recommended action. The defect list becomes the action register that maintenance teams work from — make it clear and specific.

Photographic evidence for equipment reports

Equipment inspection reports should include photos of:

  • The equipment nameplate and serial number (for identification)
  • The equipment in its installation context (overview shot)
  • The hour meter or service display
  • Each defect found — wide shot showing location, close-up showing detail
  • Any worn or damaged components removed during the inspection
  • Measurement instrument readings for critical values

Caption every photo at the time of capture. "Generator 2 — main bearing cap showing oil contamination" is a professional caption. An uncaptioned photo of a bearing cap has no value in a report.

Recommendations — make them actionable

Every defect should have a corresponding recommendation. Recommendations must be specific, not vague:

  • Vague: "Bearings require attention" — not actionable
  • Specific: "Replace main bearing set (OEM ref. [X]) within 250 running hours or 3 months, whichever is sooner. Recommend oil analysis before replacement to assess contamination extent." — actionable

Also include recommendations for preventive action where your inspection reveals systemic issues — poor lubrication practice, incorrect alignment, environmental factors causing premature wear. A good equipment inspection report improves maintenance practices, not just lists the current defects.

Frequently asked questions

How detailed should an equipment inspection report be?

Detailed enough to stand alone as a complete record if the inspector is unavailable when the report is acted upon. A maintenance engineer who reads your report six months later, without any background knowledge, should be able to understand what was inspected, what condition was found, what was measured, and what action is required. If the report does not meet that standard, it is not complete enough.

Should I include photos of components that are in good condition?

Yes, for key components. A photo of a bearing in good condition establishes a baseline. It also demonstrates that the inspection was thorough — not just a documentation of defects. For pre-purchase or pre-lease inspections, comprehensive photos of the equipment in good condition are particularly important.

How do I handle equipment I am not qualified to inspect?

State explicitly in your report the scope of your inspection and any systems or components that were outside your scope or qualification. If electrical systems require a qualified electrician and you are a mechanical engineer, note that electrical systems were excluded from this inspection and require separate assessment. Never imply broader coverage than you actually conducted.
