Insurance & Loss Adjustment Guide

How to Write an Insurance Inspection Report

For loss adjusters, marine surveyors, claims inspectors, and forensic investigators  ·  Updated May 2026

An insurance inspection report is one of the most consequential documents a professional can produce. It directly influences whether and how much a claim is paid, informs underwriting decisions, and may be used as evidence in legal proceedings. Accuracy, objectivity, and clear evidence documentation are non-negotiable.

This guide covers the structure, evidence standards, and professional practices that make an insurance inspection report credible, defensible, and useful to the parties who rely on it.

Types of insurance inspection report

  • Marine cargo damage inspection report
  • Marine hull and machinery damage report
  • Property damage inspection report
  • Fire damage inspection report
  • Flood and water damage inspection report
  • Machinery breakdown inspection report
  • Liability inspection report (third-party claim)
  • Pre-risk or condition survey report (for underwriting)

The critical rule: fact versus opinion

The most common failure in insurance inspection reports is the blending of fact and opinion without clear attribution. These two things must be kept strictly separate throughout the report:

  • Fact: "A crack measuring approximately 350mm in length was observed in the port side main deck plating at frame 42, running diagonally from upper to lower edge."
  • Opinion: "In the inspector's opinion, the crack is consistent with fatigue failure arising from excessive repeated loading."

Facts go in the Findings section. Opinion goes in the Causation or Opinion section, clearly introduced with "In the inspector's opinion" or "It is considered that…". The insurer, underwriter, or court reading your report must be able to distinguish what you observed from what you concluded.

Attribution of the insured's account: When recording what the insured told you about how the incident occurred, always attribute it clearly: "The master stated that…", "According to the Chief Engineer…", "The insured's representative advised that…". Never present the insured's account as established fact. It is their account — you are documenting it, not endorsing it.

Evidence standards for insurance reports

Photography

Insurance inspection photos must meet a higher standard than general field photos because they may be used in legal proceedings or presented to technical experts who were not present at the inspection:

  • Photograph all damage from multiple angles — orientation shot showing location, then close-up showing extent
  • Include a scale reference for size-critical findings (ruler, coin, or known object)
  • Photograph the surrounding undamaged area — this establishes that the damage is localised
  • Date and time stamps on photos should be enabled where possible
  • Caption every photo with precise location and what is being shown
  • Never edit, crop, or filter photos that will be submitted as evidence

Measurements and records

Where practical, record measurements: crack length, deformation extent, water depth, contamination area. Quantified findings are significantly more defensible than qualitative descriptions. "Extensive corrosion" is subjective. "Corrosion penetration of approximately 4–6mm depth over an area of 0.8m × 1.2m" is objective.

Documents reviewed

Always list every document you reviewed and when. If key documents were not available or refused, note this explicitly. Document availability is frequently relevant to both causation analysis and coverage assessment.

Chain of custody: For cargo inspections where samples are taken, or for inspections where evidence may later be contested, document the chain of custody carefully. Note what condition the subject was in when you arrived, who was present, and what access you had. Any interference with evidence before your arrival should be recorded.

Causation analysis — when and how

Only provide causation analysis if you are qualified to do so and have been specifically instructed to opine on cause. Many appointments are for condition documentation only — the cause is determined by a separate expert. Know which type of instruction you have before writing.

When you are instructed to opine on cause:

  • State your qualifications to opine on the specific type of casualty
  • List the facts (observations, documents, measurements) that support your conclusion
  • Acknowledge alternative explanations and explain why you consider them less likely
  • Use qualified language: "consistent with", "in the inspector's opinion", "the evidence suggests"
  • Never state cause as fact unless you can prove it conclusively

Common mistakes in insurance inspection reports

  • Presenting the insured's account as fact — always attribute statements to their source
  • Opining on cause without being instructed or qualified — if in doubt, document facts and leave causation for specialist experts
  • Insufficient photo evidence — insurers and lawyers will challenge findings that are not photographically documented
  • Missing scope limitations — if you could not inspect certain areas, say so explicitly
  • Delay in report delivery — insurance claims have commercial and legal timelines. Late reports cause operational problems and erode professional credibility
  • Omitting reservations — if new information could change your conclusions, say so. Reserve the right to revise

Frequently asked questions

Do I need specific qualifications to write an insurance inspection report?

The qualifications required depend on the subject matter. Marine hull and cargo inspections typically require a qualified marine surveyor (IIMS, RINA, or equivalent). Property and casualty inspections have different requirements by jurisdiction. All insurance inspection reports should clearly state the inspector's qualifications and the basis on which they were appointed.

Can I use AI to help write the report?

AI tools can assist with structure and grammar. However, all factual content, observations, measurements, and professional opinions in an insurance inspection report must come from the inspector's own field notes and direct observation. AI cannot attend an inspection. If AI assistance is used, the inspector remains fully responsible for every factual statement and professional opinion in the report.

How long should I retain inspection reports?

Insurance claims can give rise to legal proceedings years after the event. Retain all inspection reports, field notes, and photographic evidence for a minimum of six years, and ideally longer for high-value or complex claims. Check your professional indemnity insurance requirements.
