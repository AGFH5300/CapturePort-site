How to Write a Property Snagging Report

For property inspectors, snagging consultants, real estate professionals, and buyers  ·  Updated May 2026

A property snagging report is the formal record of defects, incomplete works, and non-conformances found during a handover inspection of a new or recently completed property. It is the document that protects a buyer's rights before they accept keys — and for snagging professionals, it is the primary deliverable that justifies their fee and demonstrates their thoroughness.

A well-written snagging report with clear photos and a numbered defect list is far more effective than a handwritten snag list when negotiating rectification with a developer. This guide explains exactly how to write one.

What is a snagging report?

A snagging report — also called a handover inspection report or defect report — documents every defect, unfinished item, and deviation from specification found in a property at the time of handover from developer to buyer. It is used to formally notify the developer of outstanding items before the buyer accepts the property.

In the UAE market, snagging is particularly important given the volume of off-plan handovers. RERA (Real Estate Regulatory Agency) in Dubai recognises the buyer's right to a defect liability period — typically one year for general defects and ten years for structural defects — but buyers must document and report defects promptly to protect their rights under Law No. 9 of 2009.

Common snagging report types include:

  • New property handover snagging report
  • Pre-handover inspection report
  • Post-rectification re-inspection report
  • Defect liability period inspection report
  • Rental property condition report (check-in / check-out)

Standard structure of a snagging report

SectionWhat it contains
HeaderReport title, reference number, date of inspection, prepared by
Property detailsAddress, unit number, floor, development name, developer, handover date
Client detailsBuyer or client name, contact details
Inspection summaryTotal defects found, breakdown by severity, areas inspected
Defect list — room by roomNumbered list of every defect with location, description, severity, and photo reference
Photo appendixNumbered, captioned photos — one per defect minimum
Overall assessmentSummary of property condition and recommendation (accept / accept with conditions / do not accept)
Signature blockInspector name, qualifications, company, date

Room-by-room inspection checklist

Entrance and common areas

  • Main door operation, seals, and hardware
  • Flooring — tiles, grout, alignment, lippage, chips
  • Walls — paint finish, cracks, efflorescence, dampness
  • Ceilings — gypsum, paint, cracks, level
  • Light fittings — installed, operational, aligned
  • Skirting boards — fitted, painted, mitre joints

Living and dining areas

  • Flooring as above
  • Walls and ceiling as above
  • Windows — operation, seals, glass condition, handles
  • Air conditioning — unit installed, remote present, drain line connected and tested
  • Power points — present, operational, correctly positioned per specification
  • TV and data points

Bedrooms

  • All items from living area
  • Wardrobes — doors aligned, shelves fitted, hinges, handles, finish
  • Balcony door and threshold

Kitchen

  • Cabinets — doors aligned, hinges, handles, finish inside and outside
  • Worktop — level, joints sealed, damage
  • Splashback tiles — grout, alignment, chips
  • Sink — secure, sealed, drainage tested
  • Appliances — installed, operational
  • Plumbing under sink — no visible leaks
  • Exhaust hood — installed, operational, ducted correctly

Bathrooms

  • Tiles — all areas: floor, walls, behind fixtures
  • Grouting — complete, no voids, appropriate colour
  • Fixtures — WC, basin, shower, bath: secure, sealed, operational
  • Taps and mixers — operational, no drips
  • Drainage — tested and flowing
  • Mirror and accessories — secure, clean
  • Ventilation fan — installed, operational
  • Hot water — confirmed at all fixtures

Balconies and external areas

  • Waterproofing and drainage
  • Railing condition and fixings
  • Floor finish
  • External wall finish visible from balcony

UAE-specific note: In Dubai, balcony waterproofing and drainage failures are among the most common post-handover complaints. Test drainage during the inspection by pouring water into floor drains and checking it clears promptly. Blocked or incorrectly laid drainage is a structural defect claim, not a cosmetic snag.

Classifying defects by severity

A professional snagging report classifies defects. Using a consistent classification makes it easier for the developer to prioritise rectification and for the client to understand what must be fixed before acceptance.

ClassDefinitionExample
CriticalSafety risk or makes the property uninhabitableExposed wiring, structural crack, gas leak, no hot water
MajorSignificant defect affecting function or valueNon-operational AC, failed drainage, water ingress
MinorCosmetic defect affecting finish qualityPaint scuff, tile chip, misaligned cabinet door
IncompleteItem specified but not installed or finishedMissing light fitting, unfinished grouting

Writing effective defect descriptions

Each defect entry should include:

  • Snag number — unique sequential reference
  • Location — specific room and position (e.g., "Master bedroom, north wall, 1.2m from floor")
  • Description — what is wrong, factually and specifically
  • Severity classification — critical / major / minor / incomplete
  • Photo reference — the photo number in the appendix that shows this defect

Example of a well-written defect entry:

Snag #047 · Master bathroom · Major
Location: Floor tile at threshold to shower area
Description: Tile lippage of approximately 5mm between floor tile and shower tray, creating a trip hazard. Grout joint is open at this junction.
Photo: 047

Photo evidence for snagging reports

Photos are the most important element of a snagging report. A numbered snag list without photos is significantly weaker when presented to a developer. For every defect:

  • Take a wide shot showing the location of the defect within the room
  • Take a close-up shot showing the defect clearly
  • Caption the photo with the snag number, room, and brief description
  • Ensure the photo is in focus and well-lit — use a torch or portable light in dark areas

For developers who will dispute defects, photos with visible defects and clear captions are far more persuasive than verbal descriptions alone.

Common mistakes in snagging reports

  • No defect numbering — without unique numbers, tracking rectification progress is impossible
  • Vague descriptions — "wall crack" is not enough. Where on the wall? How long? Horizontal or vertical? Width?
  • Missing areas — always inspect utility rooms, store rooms, parking spaces, and any storage cages included in the property
  • No severity classification — developers will prioritise cosmetic fixes over structural ones unless you classify clearly
  • Photos without captions — uncaptioned photos cannot be matched to specific snag items in a dispute
  • Accepting the property before defects are acknowledged — once keys are accepted, leverage with the developer reduces significantly. The snagging report should be submitted before or simultaneously with key handover

Frequently asked questions

How many snags should a new property have?

There is no "normal" number. A well-finished property from a quality developer might have 20–50 minor snags. A poorly finished off-plan handover can have 200+. What matters is that every genuine defect is documented. Do not self-censor to appear reasonable — your job is to find and document every deviation from the specification and acceptable finish standard.

When should a snagging inspection be done?

Ideally before you accept the keys. In practice, many buyers in the UAE accept keys and then have a snagging inspection done within the first week. Under RERA regulations, developers are responsible for defects for one year from handover (general) and ten years (structural), but defects must be reported promptly to preserve your rights.

Can I do my own snagging inspection?

Yes. A thorough buyer with a checklist and a phone can identify many defects. However, professional snagging inspectors have experience with what to look for, knowledge of acceptable finish standards, and the professional credibility that makes developers take a report more seriously. For a high-value property, a professional snagging report typically costs a fraction of the value of defects it uncovers.

Does a snagging report need to be in a specific format in the UAE?

There is no single mandated format under RERA or DLD regulations. However, your report should be on company letterhead (if from a professional snagging company), clearly dated, reference the property unit number and development name, and be signed. A numbered defect list with photos is the practical standard that developers respond to.
